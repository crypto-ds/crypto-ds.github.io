Review from last lecture

Last week, we studied message authentication codes (MACs) and digital signatures.

  • Both can be used to ensure integrity of data at rest (e.g., on your hard drive) and in transit (e.g., sent over the Internet)
  • MACs have one symmetric key \(k\) that Alice and Bob can use to send messages back and forth
  • Digital signatures are asymmetric and allow Alice (with \({\color{red}{sk}}\)) to send a message that the entire world can verify (with \({\color{green}{pk}}\))

Here is where we ended Lecture 6:

We left two problems unsolved this week.

  • MACs require that Alice and Bob agree on their symmetric secret key \(k\). How can they do so over the Internet (without meeting in person)?
  • Digital signatures require that Alice publish her public key \({\color{green}{pk}_{\text{Alice}}}\). How can everyone in the world reliably fetch this key \({\color{green}{pk}_{\text{Alice}}}\) without it being tampered with by Mallory?

We will solve both of these problems next Monday.

Learning outcomes

By the end of today’s lecture, you will learn:

  • How Alice and Bob can agree on a symmetric key, without ever meeting in person
  • How the world can reach consensus about Alice’s public key, without ever meeting her in person

7.1 Assumptions and Trust

Looking back at Unit 1: we have rapidly built up our cryptographic toolbox! But we have made some assumptions along the way.

Interactive question. Which of the following statements is not an assumption, but instead can be mathematically proved to be true based on another statement in this list?

  1. Finding collisions in SHA-256 is practically infeasible
  2. Forging HMAC is practically infeasible
  3. Factoring very big integers is practically infeasible
  4. Computing discrete logarithms in elliptic curve groups is practically infeasible

Here are some assumptions we have made so far in this course.

  • Hash function assumptions: SHA-256 is preimage-resistant, collision resistant, puzzle friendly, and acts like a random oracle
    • From this assumption, we can prove that hash-based message commitments and HMAC are secure
  • Discrete logarithm assumption: If \(G^x = Y\) and Mallory is given the two points \(G\) and \(Y\), then it is computationally infeasible for her to find the discrete logarithm \(x\)
    • From this assumption, we can prove that Schnorr and ECDSA signatures are secure
  • Bob is not Mallory: i.e., the person Alice wants to communicate with over the Internet is her friend and not her adversary

Question. Are these assumptions true?

  • Based on decades of cryptanalysis, we have strong evidence to believe that breaking SHA-256 and discrete logarithms are practically infeasible… but admittedly I cannot definitively prove these claims

  • Whether Bob is Alice’s friend is a social question, which I cannot answer for her

Avoiding intermediaries

This course covers four fundamental topics.

  1. Protecting data integrity: authenticating data and its sender over the Internet, without needing to rely on intermediaries (like Internet service providers)

  2. Protecting data availability: building consensus in financial transactions and other publicly-proclaimed statements, without needing to rely on intermediaries (like banks or social media)

  3. Protecting data confidentiality: sharing data only with specific people on the Internet, without needing to give your data to intermediaries (like big tech companies)

  4. Protecting data science: performing and verifying data analysis, without sharing your data with any intermediaries (like cloud providers or auditors)

Note the common theme across all topics:

The tools in this course allow for data-driven decision making, without the need to place blind trust in another company or person.

To be clear: it is perfectly fine to trust other people!

  • At a personal level: forming relationships is a fundamental part of being human
  • As a society: there is immense value to be gained by building civic, economic, legal, and political structures and then working to demonstrate their trustworthiness

Instead, what I claim is that:

  • It is bad if you are forced to trust a specific intermediary just to get things done
  • It is worse if you cannot verify the intermediary’s behavior to know if they are worthy of your trust
  • It is terrible if you cannot change who you trust because the intermediary is “too big to fail”

Thinking like Mallory

This course teaches you to think from an adversarial perspective, and to learn how cryptography can be used to build secure and trustworthy data science systems that allow people to:

  • Reduce the level of trust required in other entities
  • Understand exactly what they are trusting other entities to do (i.e., how things could go wrong, and what needs to happen for a bad outcome to occur)
  • Verify that trusted entities are acting correctly
  • Have free will to change which entities they trust, and for what purposes

In other words, Kerckhoffs’s principle #4 applies to people as well as data.

It must be possible to communicate and remember the key without using written notes, and correspondents must be able to change or modify it at will

It is my hope that, in this course, you develop the skill of asking (and answering) the question of “what could go wrong?” Learning to think like the adversary Mallory will help you be a better data scientist.

Question. What could go wrong with the following code? In preparing for Exam 1, try to find as many flaws with this code block as you can.

def Alice_sign(message):
   secret_key = randint(0, 2**64)                   # sample uniformly at random
   public_key = PublicKey.from_sk(secret_key)       # compute Alice's public key
   random_k   = int(time.time())                    # sample one-time randomness
   signature  = sign(secret_key, message, random_k) # run ECDSA.sign
   return public_key, message, signature            # send to Bob over the Internet

def Bob_verify(public_key, message, signature):     # received from Alice over the Internet
   return verify(public_key, message, signature)    # run ECDSA.verify, return the result

This is valid Python code (with the import statements omitted for brevity). It will execute and, if everyone is honest, Alice will send a message to Bob that successfully verifies.

But the code has multiple flaws, each of which renders the code entirely insecure and worthless in the presence of an adversary.

7.2 Diffie-Hellman key exchange

Recall that message authentication codes (MACs) allow Alice, when given any message \(m\), to append a sequence of bits called a tag that allows Bob to verify that:

  • Alice sent the message \(m\)
  • Mallory cannot perform a meddler-in-the-middle attack to tamper \(m\) in transit
Alice Mallory Bob
\(\longrightarrow\) \(\longrightarrow\)

MACs rely on a symmetric secret key \(k\) that Alice uses to Tag\({}_k\) and Bob uses to Verify\({}_k\).

  • Previously, we assumed that Alice and Bob meet one time to create the symmetric secret key \(k\)
  • Today, we will use discrete logarithms so that Alice and Bob can have authenticated communication over the Internet even if they have never physically met before

In the digital world, Alice and Bob need a secure way to exchange secrets in plain view.

“The Internet is just the world passing notes in a classroom.”

– Jon Stewart

Amazingly, this can be done!

  • This ingenious protocol was discovered in the late 1970s by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, and it is named Diffie-Hellman key exchange in their honor
  • It was based on concepts developed earlier by Ralph Merkle… the same person who invented Merkle trees
Alice Bob
\(\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad\)

Based on the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem, here is an approach that Alice and Bob can take to create a symmetric secret key.

  • Alice can choose a secret \(a\) that she keeps to herself, and send \(A = G^a\) to Bob
  • Bob can choose a secret exponent \(b\) and send \(B = G^b\) to Alice

Alice and Bob should also digitally sign their messages \(A\) and \(B\), respectively, so they can verify that they are communicating with each other. (We will resolve soon the question of how they fetch each others’ public keys.)

Now, there is a symmetric secret key that both of them can compute: \(k = G^{ab}\).

  • Alice can take the public value \(B\) and raise it to her secret exponent \(a\) to compute \(B^a = (G^b)^a = k\)
  • Bob can do the opposite: \(A^b = (G^a)^b = k\)

Moreover, nobody else can compute the symmetric secret key!

  • Mallory knows \(G\) and sees the messages \(A\) and \(B\) on the public Internet
  • But she does not know – and cannot compute – either secret exponent \(a\) or \(b\)
  • So, she has no way to compute \(k\)

The last line does not actually follow from the one before it. That is, it might be possible to compute the symmetric secret key \(k\) without learning either secret exponent \(a\) or \(b\).

Nevertheless, cryptographers do believe that the final line is true. This stronger mathematical claim is, appropriately enough, called the Diffie-Hellman assumption.

7.3 Public key infrastructure

Next, let’s turn to the problem that digital signatures require Alice to broadcast her public key \(\color{green}{pk}\), so that the rest of the world reaches consensus on which key belongs to Alice. How can we do that?

If Alice applies a digital signature to the message “I Alice hereby send $1 to Bob”, then:

  • Bob himself should become convinced that Alice was the signer
  • Bob can show it to a bank, who should also be convinced and will credit Bob’s account

Remember that the Verify\({}_{\color{green}{pk}}\) algorithm requires a public key, but it never requires Alice’s name or identity.

So technically, digital signatures convince the world that “whoever holds the secret key corresponding to \(\color{green}{pk}\) must have signed the message.”

Question. How can Alice securely bind her identity to the public key?

  • How can Alice securely tell the world: “I am Alice and my public key is \(\color{green}{pk}\)”
  • How can Alice stop Mallory from also telling the world: “I am Alice and my public key is \(\color{brown}{pk^*}\)”

There is a chicken-and-egg issue here:

  • In general, Alice can use signatures to convince the world that she’s the creator of any message, after people have associated \(\color{green}{pk}\) with Alice’s identity.
  • But to start this process, someone needs to physically check Alice’s real-world identity. Bob could do this himself, but the goal of digital signatures is to avoid the need for Alice and Bob to meet in the real world.

Certificate authorities

On the Internet, we rely on companies called certificate authorities. These companies are paid to:

  • Verify Alice’s identity (i.e., her name, email address, and/or web domain)
  • Attest to the rest of the world that Alice is the owner of the public key \(\color{green}{pk}\)

The entire system consisting of CA companies and these attestations is often called the public key infrastructure, or PKI. You can think of the PKI as similar to a telephone directory, just for cryptographic public keys rather than telephone numbers.

Let me provide two examples of digital certificates.

  1. The website bu.edu, like all websites, uses digital signatures so that all visitors know that the data is authentic and comes from BU.

    If you click on the 🔒 icon in your web browser, it shows that the certificate authority “Sectigo Limited” attests that the RSA public key B3 52 C6 ... (in hex) belongs to the organization “Trustees of Boston University” from Feb 2026 to Feb 2027.

  2. Here is a digital certificate for the website google.com. Note that Google also acts as a certificate authority, so they are attesting to their own identity. Also, this certificate expires in just 3 months, and the Google server uses ECDSA signatures with a smaller key.

Questions for discussion:

  • How can we check that the attestation is really coming from “Sectigo Limited” or “Google Trust Services”?

  • Why do certificates have expiration dates?

  • What happens if a certificate authority makes a bad attestation, like saying that B3 52 C6 ... is the public key for google.com?

7.4 Overview of consensus

In the PKI, all participants reach consensus over the binding between identities and public keys.

  • But what exactly does “consensus” mean?

  • I want to spend some time explaining how this is a deceptively powerful property. It means that everyone reaches common knowledge.

Common knowledge is a powerful concept in logic.

Consider the following statements. All of them are true, but note that each statement is strictly stronger than the previous ones!

  • I (the instructor) have black hair.
  • You know that I have black hair.
  • I know that you know that I have black hair.
  • You know that I know that you know that I have black hair.
  • (… and so on)

Muddy children puzzle

To see the power of common knowledge, let’s step away from crypto for a moment and consider the following problem.

  • There are \(n\) children who play in a playground, during which some of them get mud on their forehead (let \(m\) denote the number of muddy children, but the children do not know \(m\))
  • Afterward, the teacher gathers the children in a circle and announces that “one or more of you have mud on your forehead”
  • Each child can see if others have mud on their forehead, but cannot tell whether their own forehead is muddy
  • The children are not allowed to talk with each other

In the first minute, the teacher says: “at this moment, if you know you have mud on your forehead, please step forward.”

Question. How will the children decide whether to respond? (Note: if a child is not 100% confident that they have mud on their forehead, they do nothing.)

One minute passes. Then in the second minute, the teacher says again “at this moment, if you know you have mud on your forehead, please step forward.”

Every minute, the teacher issues the same call once again.

Question. What happens now? Remember that the children cannot talk with each other. Their only form of communication is to step forward if they are convinced that yes, they do have mud on their forehead.

Interactive question. When will the first child step forward?

  1. In minute 2
  2. In minute \(m\)
  3. In minute \(n\)
  4. No child will ever step forward

Reasoning about the puzzle

In this puzzle, the children:

  • Start with the mutual knowledge from the teacher that “one or more of you have mud on your forehead.”
  • Develop the common knowledge over time that nobody else is confident in answering the question just yet!

Specifically, the children act as follows:

  • In the first minute: if a child looks around and sees nobody with mud on their forehead, then they must be the one and only muddy child, so they step forward (and nobody else will)
  • The children’s actions in second minute depend on what happened in the first minute
    • If a child stepped forward in the first round: now everyone has common knowledge that there is exactly one muddy child, and nobody else will ever step forward
    • Otherwise, each child has learned new information: based on the fact that nobody was confident to step forward in the first round, there must be at least two children with a muddy forehead!
  • Hence in the second minute: any child who only sees one muddy forehead can be convinced that they are the second muddy child, and they will step forward
  • If nobody steps forward in the second minute, then each child has seen at least two muddy foreheads, so they conclude there must be at least three muddy children

Continuing by induction, it turns out that if there are \(m\) muddy children, then:

  • Nobody will move for \(m-1\) rounds, and then
  • In round \(m\), all \(m\) muddy children will simultaneously step forward.
  • The non-muddy children will (correctly) never step forward.

Amazingly, the children have learned so much information from each other without saying anything at all! Remember that the children did not know the value of \(m\) at the start of the puzzle.

Back to crypto

The public key infrastructure gives us a way to go…

  • From mutual knowledge of public keys: the certificate authority (CA) knows which public keys correspond to which identities
  • To common knowledge of public keys: I know which key corresponds to which entity (like bu.edu or google.com), and I know that you know the identity, and I know that you know that I know the identity, and so on

The PKI does require us to place some trust in the certificate authorities, but this trust is verifiable and revocable.

In Unit 2 of this course, we will see that:

  • Once we have achieved common knowledge about the PKI, we can build common knowledge about any other message – that is, Alice can reliably broadcast any message so anyone can read it and be convinced that everyone else can read it too

  • If there is a collection of people and most of them are honest, then we can reliably broadcast messages and achieve consensus about them without relying on a PKI
